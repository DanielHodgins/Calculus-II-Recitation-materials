    Remark 11.1. Formulas.

    • Suppose that \(0 \leq f(x) \leq g(x)\) for all \(x \geq a\text{,}\) where \(a\) is a real number. Then:
      🔗
      1. If \(\int_a^\infty g(x)\,dx\) converges, then \(\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx\) also converges.
        🔗
        🔗
      2. If \(\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx\) diverges, then \(\int_a^\infty g(x)\,dx\) also diverges.
        🔗
        🔗
      🔗
    • Arc Length Formula:
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} L = \displaystyle\int_a^b \sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2}\,dx \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • Disk and Washer Method:
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} V = \pi\int_a^b\left[R(x)^2-r(x)^2\right]\,dx \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • Cylindrical Shell Method:
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} V = 2\pi\int_a^b r(x)h(x)\,dx \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Group/Board Work:
    🔗
    Instructions: Work through each problem with your group. Be prepared to explain your reasoning on the board.

    1.

    Determine whether the improper integral converges or diverges. If it converges, find its exact value.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^3}\,dx \end{equation*}
    🔗
    What was challenging? Note what you wonder or notice as you solve the problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Rewrite the improper integral as a limit:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^3}\,dx = \lim_{b\to\infty}\int_1^b x^{-3}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Evaluate the integral:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \lim_{b\to\infty} \int_1^b x^{-3}\,dx &= \lim_{b\to\infty} \left[-\frac{1}{2x^2}\right]_1^b \\ &= \lim_{b\to\infty} \left(-\frac{1}{2b^2}+\frac{1}{2}\right) \\ &= \frac{1}{2}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the improper integral converges and its value is \(\frac{1}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Determine whether the improper integral converges or diverges. If it converges, find its exact value.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx \end{equation*}
    🔗
    What was challenging? Note what you wonder or notice as you solve the problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The integrand is undefined at \(x=0\text{,}\) so we rewrite the integral as a limit:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx = \lim_{a\to0^+}\int_a^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Evaluate:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \lim_{a\to0^+}\int_a^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx &= \lim_{a\to0^+} \left[2\sqrt{x}\right]_a^1 \\ &= \lim_{a\to0^+} \left(2-2\sqrt{a}\right) \\ &=2. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the improper integral converges and its value is \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Find the area of the region bounded by the curves \(y=x^2\) and \(y=2x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    What was challenging? Note what you wonder or notice as you solve the problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    First find the intersection points:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=2x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} x(x-2)=0, \qquad x=0,2. \end{equation*}
    On the interval \([0,2]\text{,}\) the upper curve is \(y=2x\) and the lower curve is \(y=x^2\text{.}\) Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} A &=\int_0^2(2x-x^2)\,dx\\ &=\left[x^2-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2\\ &=4-\frac{8}{3}\\ &=\frac{4}{3}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Thus, the area is \(\frac{4}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Find the area of the region bounded by the curves \(y=x^2\) and \(y=\sqrt{x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    What was challenging? Note what you wonder or notice as you solve the problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Find the intersection points:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x^2=\sqrt{x}. \end{equation*}
    The intersections occur at \(x=0\) and \(x=1\text{.}\) On \([0,1]\text{,}\) \(\sqrt{x}\) is above \(x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} A &=\int_0^1\left(\sqrt{x}-x^2\right)\,dx\\ &=\left[\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}-\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1\\ &=\frac{2}{3}-\frac{1}{3}\\ &=\frac{1}{3}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Thus, the area is \(\frac{1}{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Find the volume of the solid obtained by revolving the region bounded by \(y=\sin(x)\text{,}\) \(y=0\text{,}\) \(0 \leq x \leq \pi\) about the \(x\)-axis.
    🔗
    What was challenging? Note what you wonder or notice as you solve the problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the region is revolved about the \(x\)-axis, the cross-sections are disks. The radius is \(R(x)=\sin(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} V &=\pi\int_0^\pi \sin^2(x)\,dx. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Use the identity \(\sin^2(x)=\frac{1-\cos(2x)}{2}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} V &=\pi\int_0^\pi \frac{1-\cos(2x)}{2}\,dx\\ &=\frac{\pi}{2} \left[x-\frac{\sin(2x)}{2}\right]_0^\pi\\ &=\frac{\pi}{2}(\pi)\\ &=\frac{\pi^2}{2}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the volume is \(\frac{\pi^2}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Find the volume of the solid obtained by revolving the region bounded by \(y=e^x\text{,}\) \(y=1\text{,}\) \(x=0\text{,}\) and \(x=\ln(3)\) about the \(x\)-axis.
    🔗
    What was challenging? Note what you wonder or notice as you solve the problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the region is revolved about the \(x\)-axis, washers are used. The outer radius is \(R(x)=e^x\) and the inner radius is \(r(x)=1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} V &=\pi\int_0^{\ln(3)} \left(e^{2x}-1\right)\,dx\\ &=\pi \left[ \frac{1}{2}e^{2x}-x \right]_0^{\ln(3)}\\ &=\pi \left( \frac{9}{2}-\ln(3)-\frac{1}{2} \right)\\ &=\pi\left(4-\ln(3)\right). \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the volume is \(\pi\left(4-\ln(3)\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    7.

    Use the cylindrical shell method to find the volume of the solid obtained by revolving the region bounded by \(y=\cos(x)\text{,}\) \(y=0\text{,}\) \(x=0\text{,}\) and \(x=\frac{\pi}{2}\) about the line \(y=2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    What was challenging? Note what you wonder or notice as you solve the problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the axis of rotation is horizontal, cylindrical shells are horizontal. We therefore integrate with respect to \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solve \(y=\cos(x)\) for \(x\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=\arccos(y). \end{equation*}
    For \(0\leq y\leq1\text{,}\) a horizontal slice extends from \(x=0\) to \(x=\arccos(y)\text{.}\) Thus, the height of a cylindrical shell is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} h(y)=\arccos(y). \end{equation*}
    The distance from the axis of rotation \(y=2\) to the shell is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} r(y)=2-y. \end{equation*}
    Using the cylindrical shell formula \(dV=2\pi r(y)h(y)\,dy\text{,}\) the volume is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V=2\pi\int_0^1(2-y)\arccos(y)\,dy. \end{equation*}
    To evaluate the integral, use integration by parts. Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u=\arccos(y), \qquad dv=(2-y)\,dy. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} du=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y^2}}\,dy, \qquad v=2y-\frac{y^2}{2}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int_0^1(2-y)\arccos(y)\,dy &= \left[ \left(2y-\frac{y^2}{2}\right)\arccos(y) \right]_0^1\\ &\quad+ \int_0^1 \frac{2y-\frac{y^2}{2}} {\sqrt{1-y^2}}\,dy. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    The boundary term is zero. At \(y=1\text{,}\) \(\arccos(1)=0\text{,}\) while at \(y=0\text{,}\) the factor \(2y-\frac{y^2}{2}\) is zero. Hence,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1(2-y)\arccos(y)\,dy = \int_0^1 \frac{2y-\frac{y^2}{2}} {\sqrt{1-y^2}}\,dy. \end{equation*}
    Split the integral into two simpler integrals:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \frac{2y-\frac{y^2}{2}} {\sqrt{1-y^2}}\,dy = 2\int_0^1\frac{y}{\sqrt{1-y^2}}\,dy - \frac12\int_0^1\frac{y^2}{\sqrt{1-y^2}}\,dy. \end{equation*}
    For the first integral, use \(u=1-y^2\text{,}\) so that \(du=-2y\,dy\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} 2\int_0^1\frac{y}{\sqrt{1-y^2}}\,dy &= -\int_1^0u^{-1/2}\,du\\ &= \int_0^1u^{-1/2}\,du\\ &=2. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    For the second integral, use the trigonometric substitution \(y=\sin\theta\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} dy=\cos\theta\,d\theta, \qquad \sqrt{1-y^2}=\cos\theta. \end{equation*}
    Since \(y=0\) corresponds to \(\theta=0\) and \(y=1\) corresponds to \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\text{,}\) we obtain
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int_0^1\frac{y^2}{\sqrt{1-y^2}}\,dy &= \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Using the power-reduction identity \(\sin^2\theta=\frac{1-\cos(2\theta)}{2}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta &= \frac12\int_0^{\pi/2}(1-\cos(2\theta))\,d\theta\\ &= \frac12 \left[ \theta-\frac12\sin(2\theta) \right]_0^{\pi/2}\\ &= \frac{\pi}{4}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Consequently,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int_0^1(2-y)\arccos(y)\,dy &= 2-\frac12\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\\ &= 2-\frac{\pi}{8}\\ &= \frac{16-\pi}{8}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} V &= 2\pi\left(\frac{16-\pi}{8}\right)\\ &= \frac{\pi(16-\pi)}{4}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Thus, the volume is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{ V=\frac{\pi(16-\pi)}{4} }. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    8.

    Use the cylindrical shell method to find the volume of the solid obtained by revolving the region bounded by \(y=\ln(x)\text{,}\) \(y=0\text{,}\) \(x=1\text{,}\) and \(x=e^2\) about the \(y\)-axis.
    🔗
    What was challenging? Note what you wonder or notice as you solve the problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the axis of rotation is the \(y\)-axis, vertical slices form cylindrical shells. The shell radius is \(r=x\) and the shell height is \(h=\ln(x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} V = 2\pi\int_1^{e^2}x\ln(x)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Use integration by parts. Let \(u=\ln(x)\) and \(dv=x\,dx\text{.}\) Then \(du=\frac{1}{x}\,dx\) and \(v=\frac{x^2}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int x\ln(x)\,dx &= \frac{x^2}{2}\ln(x) -\int\frac{x^2}{2}\frac{1}{x}\,dx\\ &= \frac{x^2}{2}\ln(x)-\frac{x^2}{4} + C. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} V &= 2\pi \left[ \frac{x^2}{2}\ln(x)-\frac{x^2}{4} \right]_1^{e^2}\\ &= 2\pi \left[ \frac{3e^4}{4}+\frac14 \right]\\ &= \frac{\pi}{2}\left(3e^4+1\right). \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Thus, the volume is \(\frac{\pi}{2}\left(3e^4+1\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    Use the arc length formula to find the exact length of the curve
    \begin{equation*} y=\frac{1}{2}x^2,\qquad 0\leq x\leq1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    What was challenging? Note what you wonder or notice as you solve the problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For the graph \(y=\frac{x^2}{2}\text{,}\) the arc length from \(x=0\) to \(x=1\) is given by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} L=\int_0^1\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    First, compute the derivative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx}=x. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} L=\int_0^1\sqrt{1+x^2}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    To evaluate this integral, use the trigonometric substitution \(x=\tan\theta\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} dx=\sec^2\theta\,d\theta \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{1+x^2} = \sqrt{1+\tan^2\theta} = \sqrt{\sec^2\theta} = \sec\theta. \end{equation*}
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int\sqrt{1+x^2}\,dx &= \int\sec\theta\sec^2\theta\,d\theta\\ &= \int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    We now derive the antiderivative of \(\sec^3\theta\text{.}\) Using integration by parts, let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u=\sec\theta, \qquad dv=\sec^2\theta\,d\theta. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} du=\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta, \qquad v=\tan\theta. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta &= \sec\theta\tan\theta - \int\sec\theta\tan^2\theta\,d\theta. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Using \(\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta-1\text{,}\) we obtain
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta &= \sec\theta\tan\theta - \int\sec\theta(\sec^2\theta-1)\,d\theta\\ &= \sec\theta\tan\theta - \int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta + \int\sec\theta\,d\theta. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Adding \(\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta\) to both sides gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2\int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta = \sec\theta\tan\theta + \int\sec\theta\,d\theta. \end{equation*}
    Hence,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta = \frac{1}{2}\sec\theta\tan\theta + \frac{1}{2}\int\sec\theta\,d\theta. \end{equation*}
    It remains to evaluate \(\int\sec\theta\,d\theta\text{.}\) Multiply the integrand by \(\frac{\sec\theta+\tan\theta} {\sec\theta+\tan\theta}\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \int\sec\theta\,d\theta &= \int \frac{\sec\theta(\sec\theta+\tan\theta)} {\sec\theta+\tan\theta} \,d\theta\\ &= \int \frac{\sec^2\theta+\sec\theta\tan\theta} {\sec\theta+\tan\theta} \,d\theta. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Since \(\frac{d}{d\theta} (\sec\theta+\tan\theta) = \sec\theta\tan\theta+\sec^2\theta\text{,}\) we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sec\theta\,d\theta = \ln\left|\sec\theta+\tan\theta\right|+C. \end{equation*}
    Substituting this result into the previous expression gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sec^3\theta\,d\theta = \frac{1}{2} \left( \sec\theta\tan\theta + \ln\left|\sec\theta+\tan\theta\right| \right)+C. \end{equation*}
    Now return to \(x\text{.}\) Since \(x=\tan\theta\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \tan\theta=x \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sec\theta=\sqrt{1+x^2}. \end{equation*}
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int\sqrt{1+x^2}\,dx = \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{1+x^2} + \frac{1}{2} \ln\left(x+\sqrt{1+x^2}\right)+C. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} L &= \left[ \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{1+x^2} + \frac{1}{2} \ln\left(x+\sqrt{1+x^2}\right) \right]_0^1\\ &= \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\ln(1+\sqrt{2}). \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Hence, the length of the curve is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \boxed{ L= \frac{1}{2} \left( \sqrt{2}+\ln(1+\sqrt{2}) \right) }. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    10.

    Find the exact length of the curve
    \begin{equation*} y=\frac{1}{3}(x^2+2)^{\frac{3}{2}}, \qquad 0\leq x\leq1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    What was challenging? Note what you wonder or notice as you solve the problem.
    🔗
    Solution.
    First compute the derivative:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx} = x\sqrt{x^2+2}. \end{equation*}
    Substitute into the arc length formula:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} L &= \int_0^1 \sqrt{1+x^2(x^2+2)}\,dx\\ &= \int_0^1 \sqrt{x^4+2x^2+1}\,dx\\ &= \int_0^1 \sqrt{(x^2+1)^2}\,dx. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Since \(x^2+1>0\text{,}\) we have \(\sqrt{(x^2+1)^2}=x^2+1\text{.}\) Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} L &= \int_0^1(x^2+1)\,dx\\ &= \left[\frac{x^3}{3}+x\right]_0^1\\ &= \frac13+1\\ &= \frac43. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Thus, the exact length of the curve is \(\frac43\text{.}\)
